# Genres

Four kinds of document have rules of their own. Read only the section for the document in hand. Each section adds to the passes in `SKILL.md`; where it differs from a general rule, it wins for that genre only.

Contents: [Decision paper sent upward](#decision-paper-sent-upward) · [Results or experience report](#results-or-experience-report) · [Incident report](#incident-report) · [A message that changes people's jobs or pay](#a-message-that-changes-peoples-jobs-or-pay)

## Decision paper sent upward

A reader above the writer must approve, choose or take note: a submission to an executive or a minister, a request for budget or people, an escalated risk.

- **The opening says what the reader is asked to do:** approve, choose an option, or note. Write each request so the reader can answer yes or no: "Agree that …", "Note that …". Ask for the decision itself; a meeting is only a way to reach it.
- **A deadline carries its reason,** taken from an outside event: "for the aid to arrive before the spring fighting, a decision is needed now". A deadline without a reason drifts.
- **Each ask names an owner, a date and a size** (money, headcount or scope), so the reader can approve it as written. When the asks go to several groups, list them by group. A standing rule names its checkpoint: where it is checked and by whom, such as a question added to performance reviews.
- **The options include doing nothing,** stated fairly and with its cost. Say why each rejected option lost. Separate the decisions that can be taken now from the harder ones that can wait.
- **Money is always stated,** as a figure or as "no cost".
- **Name the approvals the reader answers for:** security, data protection, procurement. Work that sent code or data to an AI service or a vendor says where it went and who approved it.
- **Answer the objection this reader will raise,** where they would raise it. It is usually "we tried this before" or "why now": say what failed last time and what is different now. A question heading suits it ("Just some terrorist group?").
- **An escalated risk names the belief the evidence overturned,** then the event that overturned it, then the consequence in plain words, then the ask. "The accepted position was to fly without fear of failure. That changed when flight 16A eroded a secondary O-ring."
- **Name who has agreed** (concurrence, clearance), and put background in annexes.
- **Headings may be imperatives** for a reader who decides: "Resist proxies", "Embrace external trends".

## Results or experience report

Readers learn what a programme found and endorse a direction; no one is asked for a specific decision. Add decision owners and dates only if the author asks for them.

- **Open with the strongest result in the reader's terms:** who did what, in how long, against what it would otherwise take.
- **Say where code and data went** when the work used an AI service or a vendor, and under which approval. Senior readers answer for it, and will ask.
- **Translate measurements into consequences the reader can use.** A 70-foot steel tower torn from its foundations half a mile away means unshielded buildings at that distance would be destroyed.
- **Group the results by how the work is divided,** such as lifecycle stages, one result per item. The reason for a choice, such as rework that earlier choices caused, supports the stage it belongs to; it is not a result beside the others.
- **Stages without results are written forward:** the belief, the concrete intended uses, and when the evidence will come. One "We believe" carries the uncertainty for the paragraph. Use only plans the author has given. Where the draft has none, group those stages in one short forward paragraph and ask the author for the plans; empty sections with placeholders read as an unfinished report.
- **Concede before the complication,** toward readers or named competitors only. When the readers built what the report criticises, or rate a competitor it names, state their achievement or the competitor's strength as a fact first. Concede the true parts of an opposing argument ("This is true"). When the report criticises its own team, name the specific mistake: "We overhired for the world we're in." Do not credit a rejected approach with merits to look balanced; state the facts about it once, where they explain the choice.

## Incident report

- **Impact in numbers, events in time order, every time in one zone.**
- **Own the failure plainly, and name the converging causes** rather than one person.
- **Explain the technical cause with the smallest real case,** walked through step by step: a regular expression cut down to `.*.*=.*`, then the step count as the input grows.
- **Every remediation item carries its status:** done, or in progress or planned with a date.
- **Commit to measures, not outcomes.** A promise that the failure can never recur is one the reader cannot check.

## A message that changes people's jobs or pay

Layoffs, reorganisations, and changes to pay or benefits.

- **The decision and its size come in the first two sentences:** "We're reducing the size of our team by around 14%."
- **Concrete arrangements carry the message:** numbers, dates, who tells each person, and what support continues.
- **Address each group in its own section,** such as those leaving and those staying.
- **One sentence acknowledging what the reader is going through stays,** and so does a negative that answers the reader's fear ("This is not your fault"). Tell 29 and the *Negative form* entry in the checklist do not apply to these.
